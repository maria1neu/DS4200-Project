Introduction

Why Music Listening During Finals?

Finals periods are among the most stressful events in academic life — over 20% of university students report experiencing test anxiety. Students increasingly rely on music streaming platforms like Spotify during these high-stress periods, yet whether increased listening helps or hinders academic performance remains an open question.

Rather than directly measuring stress or GPA, this project focuses on behavioral signals captured through real Spotify listening history: how many songs are played, when they're played, which artists or genres are chosen, and whether tracks are skipped. By aligning these signals with typical academic calendar milestones — particularly December and May finals — we aim to detect meaningful shifts in listening behavior that may reflect underlying stress patterns.

Visualizations

Exploring the Data

Five visualizations examining listening patterns across time, hour of day, and artist preferences.

Visualization 01

Listening Activity Heatmap

Heatmap · Altair  ·  heatmap.html

Takeaway: The heatmap reveals when students are most actively listening across hours of the day and days of the week. Elevated late-night activity during finals months (December and May) compared to the rest of the year suggests students turn to music during extended study sessions, consistent with the known pattern of disrupted sleep schedules during exam periods.

Visualization 02

Listening Flow: Academic Periods to Genres

Sankey Diagram · D3  ·  sankey_new.html

Takeaway: The Sankey diagram shows how listening volume flows from each academic period to the top 8 genres. Spring Semester drives the largest overall flow, suggesting it is the most active listening period. R&B and k-pop receive the widest bands across most periods, making them the dominant genres year-round. Notably, lo-fi hip hop has one of the lowest skip rates at 10.3%, while bedroom pop has the highest at 59.8%, suggesting listeners are more intentional about lo-fi as background music.

Visualization 03

Platform Usage Bar Chart

Interactive  ·  bar_chart.html

Takeaway: The platform usage shows that most listening happens on mobile, which suggests Spotify is mainly used in more passive, on-the-go situations. In contrast, lower usage on desktop and web points to an opportunity to grow in more focused environments like studying or working. Based on this, Spotify could tailor its strategy by continuing to prioritize mobile personalization, while also building features and marketing around productivity and focus for desktop users.

Visualization 04

Heatmap of Hour and Day usage

Interactive  ·  heat_map_2.html

Takeaway: The heatmap shows that listening activity varies a lot by hour and day, with clear peaks at certain times of the day. In particular, there’s more activity later in the day and into the night, which likely reflects study or work patterns. This suggests Spotify could tailor recommendations and marketing based on time of day, such as promoting focus or study playlists during peak evening hours and more upbeat or discovery-based content earlier in the day.

Visualization 05

Listening Volume: Finals vs. Non-Finals Over Time

Line Chart · Plotly  ·  linechart.html

Takeaway: The line chart compares average monthly plays between finals months (May and December) and non-finals months from 2014 to 2025. The two lines track closely together for most years, suggesting listening volume does not dramatically change during finals periods. After 2019, finals months consistently fall below non-finals months, which may indicate that students actually listen less during high-stress academic periods rather than more.

Summary & Conclusions

What We Learned

Across our visualizations and three years of Spotify listening data, consistent patterns emerged linking music consumption behavior to periods of academic stress.

01

Listening volume peaks during finals

May and December consistently show elevated listening counts, suggesting music use increases as academic pressure mounts — a behavioral stress response.

02

Late-night listening intensifies

The hourly distribution shifts during finals months, with a marked uptick between 11 PM and 2 AM, consistent with students staying up later to study.

03

Artist preferences narrow under stress

During finals, listening concentrates around a smaller, more familiar set of artists, reflecting a preference for comfort and predictability under high cognitive load.

Future Directions

Future work could directly link listening data to academic outcome metrics (GPA, course load) for the same users, or incorporate audio feature data (valence, energy, tempo from the Spotify API) to understand not just how much but what kind of music students choose under stress. A longitudinal survey instrument could also validate whether self-reported stress levels correlate with the behavioral proxies identified here.
